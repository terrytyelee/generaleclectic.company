Thesis · Four things I learned

Two decades at the intersection of music, culture, and technology — distilled into four convictions.

Lesson 01

Talent is the entry fee. Scale is the game. Popularity that can't scale eventually loses to popularity that can.

Lesson 02

Lesson 03

Nobody falls in love with a song alone. They fall in love with the person singing it. I will always sell people and persona first.

Lesson 04

Comfort is the last stop before irrelevance. The only durable advantage I've ever had came from staying deliberately uncomfortable.

The Problem

The music industry thinks it has a quality problem. It doesn't. It has a distribution problem.

Over 100,000 new tracks hit streaming platforms every day. The vast majority will never be heard — not because they aren't good, but because nobody with reach decided to play them.

Think about it like paintings in a museum. The works on the wall aren't necessarily better than the ones in the basement. They're just the ones someone chose to hang. Fame is a function of wall space, not brushstrokes.

Traditional labels starve artists of reach. Faceless AI platforms strip them of humanity. Neither has solved the actual problem.
The Opportunity

People gravitate toward the familiar surprise. A stranger who somehow feels like a friend.

The MAYA principle — Most Advanced Yet Acceptable — has predicted cultural taste across every domain for half a century. General Eclectic owns this exact territory.

The Acceptable. Real artists. Real voices. Real scene credibility.

The Advanced. Emerging creative technology that expands reach, compresses cycle time, and multiplies presence across markets, formats, and fan touchpoints.

We use our own AGI to solve the science of hits.

The opportunity isn't to make more music. The world is drowning in music. The opportunity is to build the infrastructure that ensures great music and great artistry actually get heard.

The Scene

Hits don't start by reaching everyone. They start by being deeply loved by a connected few.

Every cultural phenomenon that looks like it exploded overnight actually grew the same way: it caught fire inside a tight-knit community first, then radiated outward. The mistake most companies make is trying to be mainstream from day one. The right move is to start with a scene.

Nu Mando

The movement

A genre-fluid, community-first sound rooted in Mandarin-language music but drawing freely from hip-hop, electronic, R&B, and global bass. Real density. Real rituals. The cultural fuel.

Chynatown

The brand

If Nu Mando is the movement, Chynatown is what you wear when you belong to it. A lifestyle and apparel IP we own and develop — visual identity, merch, events, cross-cultural collabs. Movement and brand, working as one.

That's not a niche limitation. That's the entire strategy.

The window for someone to plant a flag on this territory is open but closing. Whoever builds the permanent brand in this space in the next eighteen months will own it for the next decade.

I've spent two decades earning the right to be the one to do it. I'm not starting from zero — I'm starting from twenty years of scar tissue and a playbook I wrote in my own handwriting.
